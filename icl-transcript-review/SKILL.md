# ICL Conduct Review (FCFCOA / Family Court of WA)

Assess whether an Independent Children's Lawyer complied with their statutory role, the court-endorsed Guidelines, the court's procedural rules and their professional conduct rules — and produce a report whose every adverse finding is pinned to an exact passage of the source material and an exact provision. This is advocacy-side review work: the output must survive being handed to a judge, a Legal Aid panel manager or the Legal Services Commissioner, so precision and proportion matter more than volume.

## Why the standard is strict

The ICL is a court-appointed, publicly-funded best-interests advocate, not a party's lawyer. Three consequences drive the whole review:

1. **Impartiality is a statutory duty, not a courtesy** (s 68LA(5)(a)). Conduct that would be ordinary adversarial advocacy for a party can be a breach for an ICL.
2. **The child's views must reach the court** (s 68LA(5)(b)), and since 6 May 2024 the ICL must actually meet the child and give them the opportunity to express views (s 68LA(5A)–(5D)) unless a narrow exception applies — and if the ICL relies on "exceptional circumstances", the *court* must decide that before final orders (s 68LA(5D)).
3. **The ICL is a party for procedural purposes** (r 3.11(3) FCFCOA (Family Law) Rules 2021), so disclosure, service, subpoena and overarching-purpose obligations bind them like any party.

An ICL who complies should be recorded as compliant. Over-reach destroys the credibility of the genuine findings.

## Workflow

Work the steps in order. Each exists because skipping it has produced wrong reviews before: analysing against a superseded rule, analysing the wrong lawyer, or treating silence in a transcript as proof of breach.

### Step 1 — Verify the standards are current (every time)

Follow `references/currency_baseline.md`. Run the four quick checks (Guidelines edition; s 68LA amendments; FCFCOA Rules compilation; Barristers/Solicitors Rules amendments). Rules change on a fixed cadence (the court amends its Rules with each Family Law Amendment Act; the Legal Services Council amends conduct rules in June/July), so a baseline dated more than a few months ago may be stale.

Tell the user in one line if everything is still current. If something has changed, say what and apply the version in force **at the date of the conduct** — conduct is judged by the rule that bound the lawyer at the time, so a later tightening cannot be used retrospectively, and a later loosening does not excuse an earlier breach. Note the difference in the report's "Standards applied" section.

### Step 2 — Obtain and inventory the source material

The review needs a primary source: a transcript, a judgment, the ICL's filed documents, or correspondence. If none has been supplied, ask for it. Do not reconstruct conduct from the user's description alone — findings must cite a document.

When a document is attached:
- A PDF transcript is usually long. Read **all** of it before forming findings (use `Read` with `pages`, 20 at a time). Note the page/line convention the transcript uses (e.g. "T 42.15" = page 42 line 15) and cite in that form throughout.
- Build a short inventory first: hearing date, judicial officer, appearances (who appeared for whom, whether counsel or solicitor), the stage (interim / final / mention / Evatt List), and the documents referred to on the record (reports, subpoenaed material, affidavits). This inventory becomes the "Matter context" section and prevents mis-attributing a submission to the wrong appearance.
- Check any matter notes the user has already provided before asking for context again.

### Step 3 — Confirm the ICL's identity and status

Identify who appeared as ICL from the appearances page and the headers. Then confirm with the user — using `AskUserQuestion` — unless the user has already named the ICL in this conversation or the project file does. Two things must be settled before analysis:

1. **Who the ICL is** (the solicitor on the record and, if briefed, counsel). Where counsel appears for the ICL, in-court conduct is counsel's; out-of-court duties (meeting the child, subpoenas, case planning) are the instructing ICL's. Keep the two apart in the findings.
2. **Barrister or solicitor.** This decides which conduct rules apply: the Legal Profession Uniform Conduct (Barristers) Rules 2015 for a barrister; the Legal Profession Uniform Law Australian Solicitors' Conduct Rules 2015 for a solicitor (in NSW, Vic and WA). The two sets share the same core duties to the court — honesty, no misleading, disclosure of contrary authority, no inflaming the tribunal — but under different rule numbers. `references/conduct_rules_crosswalk.md` has the mapping. Never cite a Barristers rule against a solicitor or vice versa.

For a Family Court of WA matter, the Family Court Rules 2021 (WA) replace the FCFCOA Rules for Domain 7; the Act, Guidelines and conduct rules are the same.

### Step 4 — Cross-reference against the framework

Go through `references/compliance_framework.md` domain by domain. For each obligation record one of four classifications:

- **Compliant** — the source shows the duty being performed.
- **Breach** — the source shows the duty not performed or contravened, and the obligation is mandatory ("must").
- **Concern** — the conduct falls short of good practice, or breach is probable but the source is ambiguous, or the obligation is a "should" in the Guidelines rather than a "must" in the Act.
- **Not observable** — the duty is performed off the record (meeting the child, case plan, agency enquiries) and nothing on the record speaks to it either way. Say so; do not infer compliance *or* breach from silence.

Distinguish in-court conduct, which the transcript proves directly, from out-of-court duties, which it proves only when someone refers to them on the record (the judge asks "have you met the children?"; counsel says "the ICL has subpoenaed the school"). Those references are the evidence; quote them.

Each finding needs: the exact quote (speaker, page/line), the provision (subsection / Guidelines paragraph / rule number), the classification, and one or two sentences on why it matters. Where a statutory duty and a Guidelines paragraph both apply, cite both — the Act gives the binding force, the Guidelines give the practice standard a disciplinary body will apply.

Pay particular attention to the conduct most often found in transcripts and most often missed:

- Submissions contrary to the child's expressed views without telling the court what the views are and why the ICL departs from them (s 68LA(5)(b); Guidelines 7.10).
- Significant matters in a family report or expert report not drawn to the court's attention, or drawn selectively (s 68LA(5)(c)).
- Alignment with one party: adopting a party's proposed orders without independent analysis, echoing a party's characterisations, declining to test a party's evidence that bears on risk (s 68LA(5)(a); Guidelines 4, 6.3).
- A proposed settlement or consent position that significantly departs from what the evidence suggests is in the child's best interests, not flagged to the court (Guidelines 3.1(h), 7.12).
- Risk not raised: family violence, s 102NA cross-examination protection, interim safety (Guidelines 7.5, 7.11).
- Relying on "exceptional circumstances" to not meet the child without putting it to the court before final orders (s 68LA(5B)(c), (5D)).
- Misleading or incomplete statements to the court, or failure to correct them (Barristers rr 24–26 / ASCR rr 19.1–19.2); failure to disclose contrary authority or legislation (Barristers r 29 / ASCR r 19.6–19.8).
- Not presenting available evidence or subpoenaed material at an interim hearing where the ICL has unrestricted production-subpoena capacity (r 6.27(2)–(4); Guidelines 7.11).
- Acting after the appointment has ceased, or without a current order (r 3.11(1), (5)).

Conduct by *other* lawyers belongs in the report only where it bears on the ICL (e.g. a party's failure to serve the ICL under r 2.27(3)(b) explains why the ICL lacked a document — that counts for the ICL, not against).

### Step 5 — Check interpretation points against authority

When a finding depends on how a provision is read — what "exceptional circumstances" means, how much weight children's views carry at a given age, when an ICL's departure from views is legitimate, whether costs can be ordered against an ICL — consult `references/case_law.md` and run one targeted web search for anything newer (search FCFCOA judgments for the provision number). Cite the authority with its medium-neutral citation. Do not pad with authority the finding does not need.

### Step 6 — Write the report

Use `references/report_template.md`. The structure is fixed because the user reuses these reports as the spine of complaints and submissions:

1. **Matter context** — proceeding, hearing, ICL (confirmed), barrister/solicitor status, standards applied with versions and currency confirmation.
2. **Summary verdict** — per domain: counts of compliant / breach / concern / not observable, and a two-sentence overall assessment.
3. **Findings table** — one row per obligation examined.
4. **Critical findings** — the three to five strongest items, each with the full quote, the provision, and why it matters (weight of the ICL's submissions, disciplinary relevance, costs, appeal).
5. **Gaps and next steps** — what the source cannot show and what would resolve it (Legal Aid NSW file, ICL case plan, subpoena register, correspondence, further transcript days).
6. **Sources** — every document and authority relied on.

Tone: factual, measured, no adjectives doing the work a quote should do. Where the ICL complied, say so plainly in the summary — the report is more persuasive when it is visibly balanced.

Deliverable format follows the user's stated preferences. Unless the user asks for a file, create the report as a Docs artifact (the user keeps and reuses these); if the user asks for Word or PDF, use the matching skill. Record a one-line summary of the review (date, hearing, headline findings) in the project's lodgement/record doc if one exists and the review is for a live complaint.

## Common failure modes to avoid

- **Treating silence as breach.** A transcript rarely records whether the ICL met the child. "Not observable" plus a next-step is the right finding.
- **Citing the wrong rule set.** Check barrister/solicitor status first; a complaint citing the Barristers Rules against a solicitor will be dismissed on its face.
- **Applying today's rule to yesterday's conduct.** Use the compilation in force at the hearing date.
- **Confusing counsel's conduct with the instructing ICL's.** Attribute in-court conduct to whoever was on their feet; the ICL's accountability for counsel is a separate (supervisory) point.
- **Quoting the Guidelines as if they were binding law.** They are endorsed by the Chief Justice and applied by Legal Aid commissions and disciplinary bodies as the practice standard, but the binding duties are in s 68LA and the Rules. Frame each accordingly.
- **Over-counting.** One act can breach several provisions; report it once with all the provisions, not three times.
